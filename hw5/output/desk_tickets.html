Reflection

My take after the Problem 9 run: three tickets, five agents, two approvals signed, checking $3,400 → $736.

How would I evaluate the agents on each ticket, and why?

The agents performed well across all three tickets. They followed the logic we defined in the prompts and asked for human approval before any actual payment. No agent tried to move money on its own.

TicketHow it went
101 Bulldog teeGood. The team caught that invoice 501 already covers a reprint of this exact tee, so it didn't order a second one. It also wrote a careful reply that doesn't promise a date it can't back up.
102 Rent dueGood. It confirmed the landlord, amount and due date against the lease, checked rent wasn't already paid, and flagged that paying rent plus invoice 501 would drop us under the $500 cushion. Then it waited for me to approve.
103 Bulk hoodiesGood. It recommended a discount inside our rules ($46.40, 20% off, still 52.6% margin), sized the reprint at 12, and kept the discount and the club reply as things for me to decide.

How did the actual run compare to the expected plan?

Tickets 101 and 102: the first agent we expected was the one the boss called (Inventory for 101, Facilities for 102), and the tools we expected were used. The one exception was get_pricing on 101, which nobody needed. The biggest difference was that the boss called several agents in parallel: Customer Service alongside Inventory on 101, and Accounting alongside Facilities on 102. We had pictured one hand-off at a time.

Ticket 103: I predicted Customer Service would be called first, because that's how it would be handled in a human world: the person at the counter talks to the club first. In the actual run, Customer Service came last, after Accounting and Inventory had the numbers. That follows how our boss prompt routes discount requests.

What would have been simpler as one agent with tools, and why?

I think the rent payment would have been simpler as a separate, standalone agent with its own tools. Rent is pretty different from the actual business Campus Customs runs (selling apparel and gifts), and the rent ticket mostly needed one lease lookup, a payment check and a cash check. Passing it through the boss, Facilities and Accounting added hand-offs and tokens without adding much. It seems like there should be a standalone agent for operations outside the core business, like rent and the building, while the full team stays focused on orders, stock and pricing.

Three new problems this agent team could solve with the tools we built

Custom tees for a specific eventInventory checks stock and finds a reprint vendor and lead time. Accounting prices it and checks cash. Customer Service drafts the reply, and a human approves the purchase.
Bulk orders for a specific school (e.g. the School of Management). This works like ticket 103: stock by size, a bulk discount within our 20% / 40% margin rules, and a reprint for any shortfall.
A multi-product order from one customer, such as tees, hoodies, caps and mugs. check_stock and get_pricing work per SKU, so the team can check each item, total the order, and say what ships now and what waits.

Three new problems this agent team could not solve with the tools we built

Custom returnsThere are no tools to take stock back, issue a refund, or record a return, and no orders table to look the purchase up in.
Product complaints and ways to fix themThe team can't look up what a customer bought, and it has no way to offer a replacement, credit or refund.
Renewing the leaseWe can only read the lease and pay rent. There's nothing for negotiating terms, changing the lease, or tracking an end date.

These would need different customer service tools (orders, returns, refunds and credits, and complaint tracking), plus lease-management tools for Facilities.
